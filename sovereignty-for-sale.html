Sovereignty for Sale (Sold Separately)

Canada announces sovereignty by the billion, then quietly subsidizes a supply chain that terminates in California. The word does the work the infrastructure doesn't.

01 · The podium

The word that did the work was sovereignty

On June 4, 2026, Mark Carney stood at a podium in Toronto and launched a national AI strategy called AI for All. The government projected it would unlock $200 billion in growth and create 250,000 AI jobs, lifting Canadian AI adoption from just over 12 percent to 60 percent by 2034.

One word carried the announcement. Everything underneath it was still a procurement document.

02 · The number nobody agreed on

New money versus recycled commitments

CBC headlined the package as over $2 billion. The Prime Minister's Office framed it as up to $200 billion of growth. Independent tallies that folded existing programs into the announcement landed well north of $3.5 billion, not all of it new, some of it repackaging Budget 2025.

The ambiguity is not a footnote to the sovereignty story. It is the sovereignty story, announced from the stage before anyone left the room.

03 · The centerpiece

The supercomputer does not exist

The 2024 Sovereign AI Compute Strategy committed up to $2 billion; Budget 2025 expanded the envelope to roughly $2.4 billion. The public supercomputer, the centerpiece, runs through a program called SCIP, worth about $925.6 million.

As of November 2025, none of the fifty most powerful supercomputers in the world sat in Canada. SCIP closed applications on June 1, 2026. The winner gets eighteen months from signing to switch the machine on. The clock has not started.

The program guide notes that all funding amounts are "notional and subject to negotiation." Sovereignty-as-press-release shipped in June. Sovereignty-as-infrastructure is a build clause.

04 · What actually shipped

Foreign silicon, Canadian flag

The largest near-term commitment went to Cohere, which received up to C$240 million to build a data centre with CoreWeave, an American firm running on Nvidia silicon. The flagship sovereignty buy is capacity assembled from the supply chain it claims to escape.

The Compute Access Fund is described in its own terms as a subsidy so Canadian firms are "not entirely dependent on American hyperscalers." Not entirely dependent is the honest definition of sovereign.

05 · Run the tape on quantum

The science stays. The upside drifts south.

Canada's National Quantum Strategy is backed by $360 million over seven years. Germany committed roughly $2.8 billion CAD, France a €1.8 billion strategy. The government's own consultations warned the funding "may be insufficient to achieve the goals."

D-Wave, founded in British Columbia, is now based in Florida. Xanadu merged with a Nasdaq shell, then arranged to raise up to US$300 million from a firm tied to the Trump family. Not a clean sellout: Xanadu was more than 60 percent Canadian-owned at listing. Canada keeps the lab and rents out the upside.

06 · The portable halo

Sovereignty attaches to whatever needs it

Budget 2025 added $334.3 million for quantum, reclassified under a new Defence Industrial Strategy. The technology did not change. The funding rationale did, migrating to wherever the political appetite was strongest that fiscal year.

The word converts an industrial subsidy into a geopolitical virtue, and the conversion completes at the announcement, years before any silicon is poured. The compute strategy concedes its dependence in its own grant language. The quantum strategy filed its own insufficiency in the consultation record.

The announcement is the only part reliably manufactured in Canada.
